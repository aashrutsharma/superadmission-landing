Counselling is a matching game. You list your wishes, and an algorithm matches your rank to a seat. This guide explains what counselling is, how choice filling works, and what happens in each round of seat allotment.
Counselling in 3 ideas
You wish. You list the colleges and courses you want, from most wanted to least wanted.
Rank decides. An algorithm checks every student's rank and how many seats each college has.
You get one seat. Each student is matched to one seat, if a match is possible.
Try it: 3 students, 2 seats
Here is a made-up example to show the idea. There are three students and two seats.
Asha has rank 50. She gets the Computer Science seat.
Unnati has rank 450. She gets the Mechanical seat.
Ben has rank 900. He gets no seat in this round.
A smaller rank number is better. Rank 50 goes before rank 450.
Let's continue Unnati's journey. Her JEE Main result is out and she has a rank now. To get into college with this rank, she goes through counselling. Counselling has a lot more steps and complexity than most students expect going in.
What her result actually contains
Before registration, it is important to understand what each part of the result says. Each part gets used, or asked for, at a different place.
Overall rank or CRL: Her position against every student who took the exam, regardless of category. CRL stands for Common Rank List.
Category rank: A separate ranking calculated only among students of her specific category, such as OBC-NCL, SC, ST, EWS or PwD. It is used whenever a set of seats is reserved for that category.
Subject percentile: For some exams, a percentile specific to a subject combination. For example, a Physics-Chemistry-Maths percentile in JEE Main. It is used for eligibility on certain courses.
A student applying for a general-category seat mainly needs the overall rank. A student applying for a reserved-category seat needs the category rank instead. That is what gets compared against the reserved cutoff, not the overall rank.
Step 1: Creating the counselling profile
Registration is not a separate account. It is one combined process that works by fetching the identity a student already has from the exam.
1. Log in using exam credentials. Unnati goes to the official JoSAA website (josaa.nic.in) and clicks "Candidate Registration." There is no email-and-password signup like a typical website. She logs in with her JEE Main application number and the same password she set while registering for that exam.
2. The portal fetches her profile. The moment she logs in, JoSAA pulls her data from the National Testing Agency's own records and fills in her profile. This includes her name, date of birth, gender, category, All India Rank and state of eligibility. She does not type any of this, and none of it can be edited on this portal. If something is wrong, it has to be corrected at the exam body's end, not here.
3. She adds fresh information. A few details are not carried over from the exam:
Contact re-verification: An active mobile number and email. Every admission step requires OTPs and messages sent to these.
Bank account details: An account number, along with a cancelled cheque upload. If she withdraws her admission later, her deposit needs an account to be refunded into. The deposit is the acceptance fee paid for an allotted seat after counselling.
A new password: Separate from her exam password. It is created just for this portal and is used later to lock her list of college choices.
4. She submits and moves to choice filling. Once she submits this form, registration is complete. The portal takes her directly into choice filling.
A note for university portals like DU CSAS or IPU. Unlike JoSAA's quick login, these portals have a more detailed application form stage at this point. Before moving forward, you must type in your personal and academic information, such as Class 10 and Class 12 subject-wise marks, to verify eligibility. You also upload scanned academic and category documents, and pay a separate counselling registration fee to lock the form.
Step 2: Choice filling
This is where Unnati tells the system what she actually wants. She is shown every college and course combination. Her job is to arrange as many of them as she wants, in order, from most preferred to least preferred. Each combination on this list is called a choice.
The flow looks like this:
Open the choice filling portal.
Browse the eligible college and course combinations.
Add the preferred ones to the list.
Arrange them in order of preference.
Check if you are satisfied. If not, go back to adding and arranging.
Lock the list before the deadline.
A few things about this list are easy to get wrong.
Order matters completely. The allocation system is linear and automated. It uses a Deferred Acceptance algorithm. This is a computer program that processes students in order of rank, checks their choices from top to bottom, and holds their best possible seat until everyone is placed. It reads your list from top to bottom (1, 2, 3 and so on) and gives you the very first college and course on your list that matches your rank. It will never look at choice number 10 if you qualify for choice number 5.
More choices generally help. Leaving a college off your list does not protect you from getting it. It also does not make your top choices easier to get. It only removes the possibility of getting that college. If you do not fill enough choices, you risk ending up with no seat at all.
The list can be edited until it is locked. Nothing is final the moment it is typed in. You can change the order, add new options or delete choices as many times as you want while the window is open.
Do not mix up the mock allotment with the actual one. Many counselling processes run a mock allotment round. It is a practice trial that shows what seat you might get based on what students have filled so far. It is not your final seat. Use this data to rearrange your list before the locking deadline.
Never add a choice you dislike just to fill space. If the system gives you a seat that you listed, you cannot reject it to get a lower choice later. If you would rather sit out a year than go to a specific college, do not put it on your list.
Step 3: Locking the choices
Every round has a deadline for choice filling. Before that deadline, she can rearrange, add or remove choices as many times as she wants. Once the deadline passes, the list locks automatically, exactly as it stands. Some portals also let you lock manually before the deadline, once a student is sure.
Step 4: Seat allotment
Once every student locks their preferences, the counselling body runs its matching process against all choices and seats. It looks at four things together: the locked choice lists, the available seats, the category quotas and the rank cutoffs. The result is an allotment of a college and course for each student.
This first run is called a round. A round is a single stage of seat allocation. The computer runs its matching once, publishes the results, and gives students a few days to secure their seat. This process usually repeats three to five times to fill all seats.
Step 5: Responding to the allotment
Every round comes with a short response window. A student's decision at this stage falls into three broad categories.
Accept: Pay the seat acceptance fee, hold the seat, and choose how to take part going forward.
Decline or withdraw: Exit counselling. The seat is then allotted to someone else in later rounds. Refund rules for the deposit, if any, vary by counselling body.
No response: Missing the deadline without acting usually cancels the allotment automatically. Some systems treat this differently from a deliberate decline.
Within "Accept," JoSAA and many other counselling processes break the choice down further:
Freeze: Accept this seat and stop taking part in further rounds. This is the right choice when you are truly satisfied with the allotted seat.
Float: Keep this seat for now, but stay in counselling for something higher on your list. A better seat may later replace it automatically.
Slide: Stay at the same college, but stay open to a different course within it in a later round. The college itself is not at risk.
Exact terms and rules differ across counselling bodies. MCC, state bodies and university-specific systems word these options differently, and their refund rules for withdrawal are not the same. Freeze, float and slide are the JoSAA version, and they are a useful mental model.
Step 6: Document verification
This happens at two separate levels, even for the same student in the same counselling process.
Authority-level verification. Unnati uploads her documents online. A verification team at the counselling body reviews them. If something is rejected, she usually gets one chance to resubmit. Some systems run this before the final round of allotment.
Institution-level verification. After her allotment is final, the institute verifies her original documents when she reports to the college or university in person. This happens again, even though the counselling authority already verified copies of the same documents. A mismatch at this stage can cancel the admission entirely.
Step 7: Reporting and fee payment
To finalise admission, students complete the on-campus process:
Bring original documents. Every document you uploaded online must be shown in original for physical verification at the college counter.
Pay the balance fee. Pay the remaining college fee. The seat acceptance fee you paid earlier to the counselling board is deducted from this total.
Complete biometrics. The college office records your fingerprints and photograph for your student identity profile.
Collect your admission slip. Once verification and payments clear, the college prints your confirmation receipt. Your seat is now yours.
Rounds: why counselling repeats
Counselling never finishes in one round. Students change their minds or leave the process, so seats that were claimed become vacant again. Multiple rounds are needed to fill all the remaining spots.
Think of counselling like a restaurant waiting list. Imagine a popular restaurant where a host manages a long line of hungry people based on who arrived first. Arrival order is their rank.
Round 1: The host calls out names and fills every table in the dining room.
The shift: Ten minutes later, a few families at the front change their minds and leave to eat somewhere else. A couple of other families are called but are not at the door to answer, so they lose their spot.
Suddenly, several good tables are empty again. The host does not leave them empty. The host calls the next people in line to take those spots.
This is exactly why counselling needs multiple rounds. As people drop out or change their plans, the people behind them get a chance to step forward into a better seat.
Here is how a student moves across rounds:
Round 1 allotment. The student then decides what to do.
If she picks Freeze, the seat is confirmed.
If she picks Float or Slide, she moves to the Round 2 allotment.
If she picks Decline or No response, she exits or drops out.
After Round 2, the same decision repeats. Freeze confirms the seat. Decline exits. Float or Slide moves her to Round 3 and onward.
Most counselling bodies run between two and six such rounds. Every round repeats the same sequence.
Spot rounds
After all scheduled rounds are done, there are almost always a few seats left empty across various colleges. A spot round is a fast-track allocation round held to fill these leftover seats. It works differently from a regular round in a few ways:
Very short notice. It is announced with little warning, sometimes giving you only a day or two to register and pay the spot round fee. You have to keep an eye on the website so you do not miss it.
Tight decision windows. The time to accept a seat, upload documents or pay fees is much shorter. Instead of a week, you might get only a few hours to secure a seat before it goes to the next person.
Cutoffs are usually much lower at this stage. For this reason, a spot round can be a good chance for lower-ranked students to get a strong college that still has an empty seat.
The full cycle
Counselling registration and application
Choice filling
Choice locking at the deadline
Seat allotment
Response: Freeze goes to document verification. Float or Slide waits for the next round and goes back to allotment. Decline or no response means the student exits and the seat is released.
Document verification
Reporting, fee payment and biometrics
Admission confirmed
This is the loop Unnati goes through once for JoSAA. A student aiming for a medical seat runs through the same loop again on MCC's website. A student who wants a home-state engineering seat runs it a third time on the state's own portal.
What is being matched?
Just one counselling body, JoSAA, gives out 67,323 seats in 2026. Here is the split, based on the JoSAA 2026 seat matrix:
NITs: 25,162 seats
IITs: 18,951 seats
GFTIs (Government Funded Technical Institutes): 11,692 seats
IIITs: 11,518 seats
Seats are also shared by category. By rule, some seats are kept for certain groups. SC means Scheduled Castes. ST means Scheduled Tribes. OBC-NCL means Other Backward Classes (non-creamy layer). EWS means Economically Weaker Sections. Students in these groups also get a category rank, which is their position among students of the same category.
These are the central reservation rules as followed in JoSAA:
Open: 40.5%
OBC-NCL: 27%
SC: 15%
EWS: 10%
ST: 7.5%
Seats for persons with disabilities (5%) are counted inside each group.
In NITs, half the seats go to students from the college's own state, called Home State. The other half go to students from other states. Home State means the state where the college is located.
Who runs counselling?
There is no single body for the whole country.
JoSAA: Counselling for IITs, NITs, IIITs and other government funded technical institutes. 138 institutes in 2026.
CSAB: Runs extra rounds for NITs, IIITs and other technical institutes after JoSAA ends.
MCC: The Medical Counselling Committee runs counselling for many MBBS and BDS seats across India.
State authorities: Each state runs counselling for its own seats. These are separate from JoSAA and MCC.
There are 40+ counselling bodies in India. Each has its own website, dates and rules.
